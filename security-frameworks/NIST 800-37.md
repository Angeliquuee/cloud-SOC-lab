
## What is RMF?

A framework for managing the risk of information systems. The point of it is to accept the right amount of risk though not too much and not zero which is impossible. It helps a system owner figure out what level of risk they are willing to accept.

> "System" can mean a few different things:
> MACRO - An Entire Organization
> MID - A piece of software (Saas, app)
> MICRO - A single feature in an app


## When do you use RMF?

*Whenever sensitive info is involved like personal data, financial info, business secrets*

Common triggers include building or buying a new system, after a security breach, and/or making changes to a system.

## How do you use RMF?

NIST lays out a 6-step process. Same steps, everytime.

1. Categorize - *How sensitive is the system?*
2. Select - *Which controls do we need?*
3. Implement - *Actually build & turn them on.*
4. Assess - *Check that they really work.*
5. Authorize - *A leader signs off on the risk.*
6. Monitor - *Keep watching. Forever.*

## Step 1: Categorize 

> Example: A non-mission-critical app where city residents look up public information.

How would you rate the example? We need to rate the system based on the CIA triad: how much would it hurt if each was violated?

**Confidentiality:** Low (It's public info)
**Integrity:** Medium (Needs to be accurate, but easy to fix)
**Availability:** Low (Not mission-critical)

### Regulated Data

If your system stores or processes regular data, that affects which controls you need.

Examples of regulated data:
**PCI DSS** - Cardholder / Credit Card Data
**HIPAA** - Electronic Protected Health Info (ePHI)
**GDPR** - Personal data from anyone in the EU

## Step 2: Select Controls

Pick the security controls that make sense for your system. The controls come from **NIST 800-53**.

### Control families (sample):
* AC - Access Control
* AT - Awareness Training
* AU - Audit & Accountability 
* CA - Assessment, Authorization, Monitoring
* CM - Configuration Management
* IA - Identification & Authentication
* IR - Incident Response
* PE - Physical & Environmental
* SC - System & Communications Protection

> There are 20 families total

#### Control families (example):

**AC-7 Unsuccessful Logon Attempts**
Enforce a limit of consecutive failed login attempts during a defined time window. Automatically lock the account, or delay the next login attempt, when the limit is reached.

**AC-11 Device Lock**
Prevent further access to the system after a defined period of inactivity. Require the user to re-authenticate (with a password, smartcard, biometric, etc.) to unlock.

## Step 3: Implement Security Controls

**Controls can take time as they are written generally**, but every system is different. The implementation of Account Lockout on a SaaS app on a custom-built API look totally different in practice.

**It can require skills and money**. Some controls aren't just config CA-8(2) is "Red Team Penetration Testing" that means hiring real attackers to try to break in. Not cheap.

**It's not always a button**. Sometimes engineers have to build the control into the system. Off-the-shelf products usually have many controls built in like an Apple iPhone for instance.

**Don't break the thing while securing it**. Make sure the controls don't cripple the functionality the system was built for. Security that no one can use isn't security.

## Step 4: Assess Security Controls

A Security Assessor (you, or an outside auditor) checks that the controls really work.

### How the conversation goes

> "Ok, I see your list of what you said is implemented. Now let me actually verify"

> "Show me this. Show me that."

> "This isn't quite implemented as described. Please fix it and get back to me."

> "All findings are resolved. All controls are in place as described."

## Step 5: Authorize the System

The **Authorizing Official (AO)** formally documents and **accepts** the residual risk in the system.

## Step 6: Monitor Controls & System

On-going cyber hygiene. The job is not done at sign off.

New vulnerabilities discovered? Remediate them.
New threats showing up? Respond to them.
System changing? Make sure it doesn't increase risk. Get AO sign-off.

>*Goal is to keep the risk at the level the AO accepted or lower*

## NIST SP 800-37 Revision 2

NIST added **Prepare** as a formal step in Revision 2 of NIST SP 800-37 published in December 2018.

#### 1. Prepare - Get Ready
Identify the organization's mission, priorities, responsibilities, and risk tolerance. Establish the context for managing security and privacy risks before working through the system-specific steps.

**Example**: A company identifies which business systems are critical and who is responsible for protecting them.

#### 2. Categorize - Understand the impact
Determine how sensitive the information is and how much damage could occur if its confidentiality, integrity, or availability was compromised.

**Example**: A system storing PCI DSS data may have a high impact if that data is exposed.

#### 3. Select - Choose Security Controls
Select appropriate safeguards based on the system categorization, risk, assessment, and applicable requirements. NIST SP 800-53 provides a catalog of security and privacy controls.

**Example**: Select MFA, access controls, encryption, and audit logging.

#### 4. Implement - Put Controls in place
Configure and deploy the selected security controls, then document how they operate in the system.

**Example**: Enable MFA, configure firewall rules, and turn on security logging.

#### 5. Assess - Test the controls
Evaluate whether the controls are implemented correctly, operate as intended, and produce the required security outcomes.

**Example**: Test MFA enforcement, review audit logs, and check for misconfigured permissions.

#### 6. Authorize - Make the risk decision
An authorized senior official reviews the assessment results and decides whether the remaining risk is acceptable for the system to operate.

**Example**: An authorizing official grants an Authorization to Operate (ATO), potentially with conditions, or declines authorization until risks are addressed.

#### 7. Monitor - Keep checking
Continuously monitor security controls, system changes, new vulnerabilities, and emerging threats to ensure risks remain acceptable.

**Example**: Use Microsoft Sentinel to investigate alerts and Tenable to identify newly discovered vulnerabilities.

## Interview Questions

> What is the Risk Management Framework (RMF), and what is its purpose?

The Risk Management Framework is a structured process that is used to identify, assess, and manage security and privacy risks associated with information systems. Its purpose is to protect an organization's systems and data by implementing appropriate security controls and continuously monitoring their effectiveness.

The goal isn't necessarily to eliminate all risk, because that is often impractical or impossible. Instead, the goal is to reduce risk to an acceptable level while balancing security requirements with the system's functionality, usability, and operational needs.

> Key differences between RMF and the previous certificate & accreditation (*C&A*) process? 

The key difference between the Risk Management Framework (RMF) and the legacy Certification and Accreditation (C&A) process is that RMF shifts the focus from a point-in-time security approval to continuous, life-cycle approach to risk management.

Under the legacy C&A process, systems underwent security certification to verify that security requirements were met, following by accreditation, where an authorized official accepted the risk and approved the system for operation. Although security reviews could be repeated, the process was often treated as a periodic compliance exercise.

RMF modernized this approach by introducing a structured, seven-step process: Prepare, Categorize, Select, Implemented, Assess, Authorize, and Monitor. It emphasizes selecting controls based on risk, assessing their effectiveness, making explicit risk-based authorization decisions, and continuously monitoring the system as threats, vulnerabilities, and configurations change.

Another important distinction is that RMF integrates security and privacy risk management into the system development life cycle, rather than treating security approval as a final checkpoint.

Ultimately, C&A focused on certifying security controls and accrediting the system, whereas RMF provides a more comprehensive, going process for managing risk throughout the system's life cycle. The goal is not simply to achieve compliance, but to maintain an informed understanding of risk and ensure that it remains within the organization's acceptable tolerance.

> What is the role of the system owner in the RMF process?

The System Owner plays a critical role in the Risk Management Framework because they are responsible for the overall operation, maintenance, and security of an information system throughout its life cycle.

Their responsibilities include ensuring the system is properly categorized, coordinating the selection and implementation of security controls, working with security teams to assess those controls, and ensuring that security documentation is maintained and up to date. 

The System Owner also coordinates with stakeholders such as Information System Security Officer (ISSO), security control assessors, and system administrators to address vulnerabilities, track remediation efforts, and ensure the system meets applicable security requirements.

Once the controls have been assessed, the System Owner supports the authorization process by providing the necessary documentation and helping the Authorizing Official understand the system's security posture and remaining risks.

An important distinction is that the System Owner manages the system and supports the risk management process, while the Authorizing Official makes the formal decision to accept the residual risk and authorize the systems to operate.

Ultimately, the System Owner helps ensure that security is built into the system throughout its life cycle rather than treated as a one-time compliance requirement.
